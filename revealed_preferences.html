5. Consumer’s Choices: Revealed Preferences

Weak, Strong, and Generalized Axioms of Revealed Preferences (WARP, SARP, GARP), Afriat’s Theorem, money pump measures of rationality, and experimental consistency.

1. How Does It Work?
The Revealed Preference Logic:
Let $(x_1, x_2)$ and $(y_1, y_2)$ be two bundles, and $(p_1, p_2)$ be prevailing prices, such that:
$$p_1 x_1 + p_2 x_2 \geq p_1 y_1 + p_2 y_2$$
We observe that the consumer has chosen to buy $(x_1, x_2)$.
What can you infer about this consumer’s preferences?
She bought $X$, while $X$ was more expensive than $Y$. It means that she had the budget to buy either $X$ or $Y$. Assuming that she is choosing her preferred bundle, we can infer:
$$(x_1, x_2) \succeq (y_1, y_2)$$
If the consumer buys a bundle instead of another affordable one, then the first bundle is revealed preferred to the second.
Revealed Preferred vs. Strictly Revealed Preferred
Consider $(y_1, y_2)$ a different bundle from $(x_1, x_2)$:
  • Revealed Preferences: $(x_1, x_2)$ is revealed preferred to $(y_1, y_2)$ iff:
    $$X \text{ is chosen and } p_1 x_1 + p_2 x_2 \geq p_1 y_1 + p_2 y_2$$
  • Strict Revealed Preferences: $(x_1, x_2)$ is strictly revealed preferred to $(y_1, y_2)$ iff:
    $$X \text{ is chosen and } p_1 x_1 + p_2 x_2 > p_1 y_1 + p_2 y_2$$
    Bundle $Y$ was strictly inside the interior of the affordable budget set.
2. Weak Axiom of Revealed Preferences (WARP)
An Example & Motivation:
The previous intuition relies on the assumption that the consumer is always choosing the most preferred bundle she can afford, i.e. maximizing her utility.
What kind of observation would lead us to conclude that the consumer is not following the utility-maximizing model?
An observation where $(x_1, x_2)$ is chosen for one budget, and $(y_1, y_2)$ is chosen for the other budget, even though both were affordable in both situations.
Definition — Weak Axiom of Revealed Preference (WARP)
If a consumer’s observed choices come from maximizing a strictly quasi-concave utility function under budget constraint, then these observed choices satisfy WARP:
  • If a bundle $(x_1, x_2)$ is purchased at prices $(p_1, p_2)$
  • And if a different bundle $(y_1, y_2)$ is purchased at prices $(q_1, q_2)$
then if:
$$p_1 x_1 + p_2 x_2 \geq p_1 y_1 + p_2 y_2 \quad\text{i.e., } X^p \text{ RP } Y^p \tag{1}$$
it must not be the case that:
$$q_1 y_1 + q_2 y_2 \geq q_1 x_1 + q_2 x_2 \quad\text{i.e., } Y^q \text{ RP } X^q \tag{2}$$
Alternative Definition — WARP (bis)
If a bundle $(x_1, x_2)$ is chosen at prices $(p_1, p_2)$ and a different bundle $(y_1, y_2)$ is chosen at prices $(q_1, q_2)$, then if:
$$p_1 x_1 + p_2 x_2 \geq p_1 y_1 + p_2 y_2 \tag{3}$$
it must be the case that:
$$q_1 y_1 + q_2 y_2 < q_1 x_1 + q_2 x_2 \tag{4}$$
Theorem & Proof: Utility Maximization and WARP
If a consumer’s observed choices come from maximizing a strictly quasi-concave utility function under budget constraint, then these observed choices satisfy WARP.
1
Strictly quasi-concave utility + convex budget set $\implies$ at most one utility-maximizing bundle for every price.
2
Let $X^0$ maximize utility facing $p^0$ and $X^1$ maximize utility facing $p^1$, with $X^0 \neq X^1$.
3
Suppose $X^0 \text{ RP } X^1$, which means that $p^0 \cdot X^0 \geq p^0 \cdot X^1$.
Hence, $u(X^0) > u(X^1)$ because the consumer maximizes a strictly quasi-concave utility (unique maximum).
4
When $X^1$ is chosen facing prices $p^1$, bundle $X^0$ cannot have been affordable; it must therefore be that:
$$p^1 \cdot X^0 > p^1 \cdot X^1 \iff p^1 \cdot X^1 < p^1 \cdot X^0$$
Choices satisfy WARP. $\blacksquare$
Utility Maximization vs. WARP: Does WARP $\implies$ Utility Maximization?
We have seen that: $\text{Utility Maximization} \implies \text{Choices satisfy WARP}$.
Can we say that: $\text{Choices satisfy WARP} \implies \text{Utility Maximization}$?
Under standard consumer-demand assumptions:
  • Yes, if there are only two goods ($n = 2$).
  • No, otherwise ($n \geq 3$).
How must we strengthen WARP to obtain a theory of revealed preference equivalent to utility maximization?
We must account for indirect chains of choices through GARP!
3. Strong & Generalized Axioms (SARP & GARP)
Directly RP vs. Indirectly RP
We now make the formal distinction between:
  • Directly Revealed Preferences (DRP): Consider $(y_1, y_2)$ a different bundle from $(x_1, x_2)$.
    $(x_1, x_2)$ is directly revealed preferred to $(y_1, y_2)$ ($X \text{ DRP } Y$) iff:
    $$p_1 x_1 + p_2 x_2 \geq p_1 y_1 + p_2 y_2$$
  • Indirectly Revealed Preferences (IRP): Consider $(z_1, z_2)$, $(y_1, y_2)$, and $(x_1, x_2)$ three different bundles.
    If $(y_1, y_2)$ is directly revealed preferred to $(z_1, z_2)$, and $(x_1, x_2)$ is directly revealed preferred to $(y_1, y_2)$, then $(x_1, x_2)$ is indirectly revealed preferred to $(z_1, z_2)$:
    $$X \text{ DRP } Y \quad\text{and}\quad Y \text{ DRP } Z \implies X \text{ IRP } Z$$
Strong Axiom of Revealed Preference (SARP)
If $x^1 \text{ DRP } x^2, \dots, x^{K-1} \text{ DRP } x^K$ with $x^1 \neq x^K$ ($x^1 \text{ IRP } x^K$), then it must not be the case that $x^K \text{ DRP } x^1$. SARP extends WARP to indirect sequences. It requires strictly single-valued choices (ruling out indifference / multiple optimal bundles).
Definition — Generalized Axiom of Revealed Preference (GARP)
For any sequence of bundles $\{x^1, \dots, x^K\}$ chosen at prices $\{p^1, \dots, p^K\}$, if:
$$x^1 \text{ DRP } x^2, \; x^2 \text{ DRP } x^3, \; \dots, \; x^{K-1} \text{ DRP } x^K \quad (\text{i.e. } x^1 \text{ is IRP to } x^K)$$
then it must be the case that:
$$x^K \text{ is NOT strictly revealed preferred (SRP) to } x^1 \quad (\text{i.e. } p^K \cdot x^1 \geq p^K \cdot x^K)$$
Intuition: This prevents cycles of revealed preferences, including those that arise indirectly through chains of choices. Unlike SARP, GARP allows flat indifference curves and multi-valued choices.
WARP vs. GARP: The Implication Relation
$$\boxed{\text{GARP} \implies \text{WARP}}$$
  • WARP considers direct revealed preferences...
  • GARP is the same but considers both direct and indirect preferences.
  • Only two bundles: No difference, because indirect chains cannot form.
  • More than two bundles: GARP can detect inconsistencies in chains of choices, whereas WARP cannot.
1
From the assumption on utility: By local non-satiation, every neighborhood of a bundle contains another bundle giving a strictly higher utility.
2
Let $x^k$ maximize utility facing $p^k$ for all $k = 1, \dots, K$.
3
Suppose $p^j \cdot x^{j+1} \leq p^j \cdot x^j$, for all $j \leq K - 1$.
Deduce: $u(x^1) \geq u(x^2) \geq \dots \geq u(x^K)$.
4
Suppose that $p^K \cdot x^1 < p^K \cdot x^K$ ($x^K \text{ SRP } x^1$).
From local non-satiation, we can find a bundle $\tilde{x}$ close to $x^1$ with:
$$p^K \cdot \tilde{x} < p^K \cdot x^K \quad\text{and}\quad u(\tilde{x}) > u(x^1) \geq u(x^K)$$
$\implies x^K$ cannot maximize utility at $p^K$, which is a contradiction! Therefore choices satisfy GARP. $\blacksquare$
Afriat’s Theorem (1967)
The following statements are equivalent:
  1. A finite dataset $D$ satisfies GARP.
  2. A finite dataset $D$ can be rationalized by a locally non-satiated utility function.
  3. A finite dataset $D$ can be rationalized by a continuous, concave, and strictly monotone utility function.
Intuition: GARP is necessary and sufficient to treat the consumer as if (s)he is maximizing a well-behaved utility function.
4. Measuring Violations of GARP & Money Pump
While GARP provides a binary outcome — either the data satisfies it or it does not — it is important to measure the severity of violations.
Numerical Example with 2 Bundles at 2 Time Periods
Consumer’s violation of GARP:
  • At prices $p_A = (10, 15)$, the consumer chooses $A = (5, 5)$, although $B = (8, 2)$ is affordable:
    $$p_A \cdot A = 125 > 110 = p_A \cdot B$$
  • Later, at prices $p_B = (14, 12)$, the consumer chooses $B$, although $A$ is affordable:
    $$p_B \cdot B = 136 > 130 = p_B \cdot A$$
Hence, $A$ is revealed preferred to $B$ and $B$ is revealed preferred to $A$: $\implies$ violation of GARP.
What is the Economic Cost of the Inconsistency?
  • At $p_A$, the consumer is willing to spend 15 more on $A$ than on $B$.
  • At $p_B$, the consumer is willing to spend 6 more on $B$ than on $A$.
This generates a money-pump cost of:
$$mp = p_A \cdot (A - B) + p_B \cdot (B - A) = 15 + 6 = 21$$
Intuition: The consumer is willing to sacrifice resources to move from $B$ to $A$ under the first prices, and then to move from $A$ back to $B$ under the second prices. The cycle $B \to A \to B$ leaves the consumer with the same bundle but creates a monetary loss since (s)he is willing to spend resources at both steps.

Money Pump Index (MPI)
The money pump index measures the potential monetary loss a consumer could incur due to violations of GARP (i.e., “cost” of being irrational in purchasing decisions).

Given a sequence $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n$ of chosen bundles ($n$ observations), given a violation of GARP such that $x_1 \text{ RP } x_2 \text{ RP } \dots \text{ RP } x_n$ and $x_n \text{ RP } x_1$:
  • The money pump cost is:
    $$mp = \sum_{h=1}^n p_h \cdot (x_h - x_{h+1}) \quad (\text{taking } n+1 = 1)$$
  • The money pump index is a normalization of the cost as a proportion of total expenditure:
    $$mpi = \frac{mp}{\sum_{h=1}^n p_h \cdot x_h}$$

MPI provides a measure of the economic impact of irrational consumption behavior.
Application to household-level food consumption data (panel data):

  • About 80% of households violate GARP at least once.
  • These violations represent on average 6% of household food expenditure $\implies$ small violations, consistent with rationality + measurement errors.
5. Experimental Insights: Altruism, Mistakes & Demographics
Is Altruism Rational?
We observe many instances of non-selfish behavior; some economists are tempted to qualify this behavior as “irrational”. Yet, altruism can be consistent with the economic definition of rationality if it can be expressed as a well-behaved preference ordering.

Experimental Design:
  • Participants could allocate their budget into their own account or a stranger’s account.
  • The “price” of 1 in own account ($\pi_o$) is 1, while the “price” of 1 in stranger account ($\pi_s$) varies.

Results:
  • 10% of participants violated WARP or GARP.
  • For the other 90%: the behavior of about half of participants was consistent with standard utility functions:
    $$u(\pi_s, \pi_o) = \pi_o \quad (\textbf{Selfish})$$ $$u(\pi_s, \pi_o) = \min\{\pi_s, \pi_o\} \quad (\textbf{Equal Split / Leontief})$$ $$u(\pi_s, \pi_o) = \pi_s + \pi_o \quad (\textbf{Perfect Substitutes: give everything if price } < 1, \text{ keep otherwise})$$
Implications: Altruistic behavior is consistent with economic rationality. Altruism is predictable and can be understood using standard economic models.
Are We Irrational by Mistake?
Studies observe many violations of rationality. Are these violations intentional deviations from the axioms, or are they simply mistakes?
A violation is a mistake when an individual violates an axiom but would not have done so had they known they were violating the axiom.
Experimental Design:
  1. Present participants with axioms (can choose that the algorithm governs choices, or choose on their own).
  2. Present participants with choice problems.
  3. Present participants with inconsistencies in their preferences (can change choice, stop using algorithm, or neither).

Results:
  • Around 85% of subjects desire an axiom to make choices on their behalf.
  • When presented with their inconsistencies:
    • 47% solve inconsistency by changing their choices
    • 13% solve inconsistency by stopping to use the axiom algorithm
    • 40% keep their choices & continue to let the axiom choose

Implications: Violations of canonical axioms stem mainly from mistakes; hence we can maintain confidence in the normative appeal of the theory, despite the fact that it can be descriptively inaccurate.
Are We More Rational with Age? Afriat’s CCEI
Afriat’s Critical Cost Efficiency Index (CCEI) is the largest fraction $\varepsilon \in [0, 1]$ such that, if you shrink all observed budgets by $\varepsilon$, the choices become consistent with GARP.
Interpretation: It is the fraction of the budget that is consistent with rational behavior:
  • $\text{CCEI} = 1 \implies$ Choices are perfectly consistent with GARP.
  • $\text{CCEI} = 0.9 \implies$ Choices could be rationalized by a utility function if the budget were adjusted by at most 10%.
Intuition: Scaling by $\varepsilon < 1$ reduces the size of the budget set you assume the consumer faced. This makes their chosen bundle look more rational, because fewer alternatives are now affordable.

Results across age (Sample of 7 yo, 11 yo, and 21 yo):
  • Proportion with zero GARP violations:
    • About 25% of 7 yo
    • About 60% of 11 yo
    • About 65% of 21 yo
  • Afriat CCEI is around 0.95 in all age groups.
Implications: Since children’s choices seem rational by age 11, it can be valid to use data from children's decisions to infer preferences. Cognitive development matters for rational choice; cognitive training can improve decision-making in adulthood.
Who is More Rational? Socio-Demographic Heterogeneity
Sample of 1,182 individuals (Dutch households) choosing allocations in 25 decision problems under risk.
Consistency measured using Afriat's Critical Cost Efficiency Index.

Results:
  • Low-income subjects “leave on the table” more of their earnings relative to high-income subjects: 3.3 percentage points.
  • Low-educated: 2.6 pp.
  • Old: 5.1 pp.

Implications: Consistency in laboratory experiments reflects decision-making ability impacting real-world outcomes. There exists a large heterogeneity in choice consistency, which may justify soft “paternalism”.
