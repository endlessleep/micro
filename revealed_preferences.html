5. Consumer’s Choices: Revealed Preferences

WARP, GARP, Afriat’s Theorem, empirical money pump measures of rationality, and experimental consistency.

1. How Revealed Preference Works
The Revealed Preference Logic:
Let $(x_1, x_2)$ and $(y_1, y_2)$ be two consumption bundles, and let $(p_1, p_2)$ be prevailing market prices such that:
$$p_1 x_1 + p_2 x_2 \geq p_1 y_1 + p_2 y_2$$
Suppose we observe that the consumer chooses to purchase $(x_1, x_2)$.
What can we infer about the consumer's preferences?
She bought $X$ when $X$ was at least as expensive as $Y$. This means she had the budget to purchase either $X$ or $Y$. Assuming that she always selects her most preferred affordable alternative, we can legitimately infer:
$$(x_1, x_2) \succeq (y_1, y_2)$$
Fundamental principle: If a consumer buys a bundle instead of another affordable bundle, the purchased bundle is revealed preferred to the affordable one.
Revealed Preferred vs. Strictly Revealed Preferred
Let $X = (x_1, x_2)$ and $Y = (y_1, y_2)$ be two distinct bundles ($X \neq Y$):
  • Revealed Preferred ($X \text{ RP } Y$): $X$ is purchased at prices $p$ and:
    $$p_1 x_1 + p_2 x_2 \geq p_1 y_1 + p_2 y_2$$
    $Y$ was weakly affordable when $X$ was bought.
  • Strictly Revealed Preferred ($X \text{ SRP } Y$): $X$ is purchased at prices $p$ and:
    $$p_1 x_1 + p_2 x_2 > p_1 y_1 + p_2 y_2$$
    $Y$ was strictly cheaper than $X$ (strictly inside the budget set) when $X$ was bought. Under local non-satiation, this guarantees that $X \succ Y$.
2. Weak Axiom of Revealed Preferences (WARP)
The Test of Rationality: The revealed preference intuition relies on the assumption that the consumer maximizes a stable preference ordering under a budget constraint.
What kind of observation would lead us to conclude that the consumer violates utility maximization?
If the consumer chooses $X$ when $Y$ was affordable, but in another price situation chooses $Y$ when $X$ was affordable, her behavior is inconsistent: she reveals $X \succ Y$ and $Y \succ X$ simultaneously!
Definition — Weak Axiom of Revealed Preference (WARP)
Let bundle $X = (x_1, x_2)$ be chosen at prices $p = (p_1, p_2)$, and let a different bundle $Y = (y_1, y_2)$ ($X \neq Y$) be chosen at prices $q = (q_1, q_2)$.
If:
$$p_1 x_1 + p_2 x_2 \geq p_1 y_1 + p_2 y_2 \quad (X \text{ RP } Y)$$
then it must NOT be the case that:
$$q_1 y_1 + q_2 y_2 \geq q_1 x_1 + q_2 x_2 \quad (\text{i.e. } Y \text{ RP } X \text{ is forbidden})$$
Alternative Definition — WARP (bis)
Equivalently, if bundle $X$ is chosen at prices $p$, and bundle $Y \neq X$ is chosen at prices $q$:
$$p_1 x_1 + p_2 x_2 \geq p_1 y_1 + p_2 y_2 \implies q_1 y_1 + q_2 y_2 < q_1 x_1 + q_2 x_2$$
If bundle $X$ was revealed preferred to $Y$, then at the prices $q$ where $Y$ was purchased, bundle $X$ must have been strictly unaffordable.
Theorem: Utility Maximization $\implies$ WARP
If a consumer's observed choices come from maximizing a strictly quasi-concave utility function under a linear budget constraint, then these observed choices satisfy WARP.
1
Uniqueness of the Optimum:
A strictly quasi-concave utility function maximized over a convex budget set produces a strictly unique utility-maximizing bundle for every positive price vector.
2
Set up the Choices:
Let $X^0$ maximize utility subject to prices $p^0$ (so $p^0 \cdot X \leq p^0 \cdot X^0$), and let $X^1$ maximize utility subject to prices $p^1$ (so $p^1 \cdot X \leq p^1 \cdot X^1$), with $X^0 \neq X^1$.
3
First Budget Comparison:
Suppose $X^0 \text{ RP } X^1$, meaning $p^0 \cdot X^0 \geq p^0 \cdot X^1$. Since $X^1$ was affordable under budget $p^0$ and $X^0$ was the unique global maximizer:
$$u(X^0) > u(X^1)$$
4
Second Budget Implication:
Now consider prices $p^1$ where $X^1$ was chosen. If $X^0$ were affordable at prices $p^1$ (i.e. if $p^1 \cdot X^0 \leq p^1 \cdot X^1$), the consumer would have purchased $X^0$ instead of $X^1$ because $u(X^0) > u(X^1)$.
Since she actually purchased $X^1$, bundle $X^0$ must have been strictly unaffordable:
$$p^1 \cdot X^0 > p^1 \cdot X^1 \iff p^1 \cdot X^1 < p^1 \cdot X^0$$
Thus, $X^1 \text{ RP } X^0$ is impossible. The choices satisfy WARP. $\blacksquare$
Limitation of WARP: Does Choices Satisfy WARP $\implies$ Utility Maximization?
We know that Utility Maximization $\implies$ WARP. Can we reverse the implication?
  • Yes, if there are only two goods ($n = 2$): Pairwise comparisons are exhaustive; indirect cycles cannot form.
  • No, if there are three or more goods ($n \geq 3$): WARP only evaluates direct pairwise comparisons. It cannot rule out inconsistencies that arise through chains of choices ($A \text{ RP } B$, $B \text{ RP } C$, but $C \text{ RP } A$).
To obtain an equivalence between revealed preferences and utility maximization for arbitrary numbers of goods, we must strengthen WARP to GARP!
3. Generalized Axiom of Revealed Preferences (GARP)
Directly RP vs. Indirectly RP
  • Directly Revealed Preferred (DRP): Bundle $X$ is directly revealed preferred to bundle $Y$ ($X \text{ DRP } Y$) iff:
    $$p \cdot X \geq p \cdot Y$$
    where $p$ is the price vector at which $X$ was chosen.
  • Indirectly Revealed Preferred (IRP): Consider three or more bundles $X, Y, Z$. If $X \text{ DRP } Y$ and $Y \text{ DRP } Z$, then by transitivity of revealed preference:
    $$X \text{ IRP } Z$$
    More generally, if there exists a sequence $\{x^1, x^2, \dots, x^K\}$ such that $x^1 \text{ DRP } x^2 \text{ DRP } \dots \text{ DRP } x^K$, then $x^1$ is indirectly revealed preferred to $x^K$.
Definition — Generalized Axiom of Revealed Preference (GARP)
For any finite sequence of bundles $\{x^1, x^2, \dots, x^K\}$ chosen at respective prices $\{p^1, p^2, \dots, p^K\}$:
If:
$$x^1 \text{ DRP } x^2, \; x^2 \text{ DRP } x^3, \; \dots, \; x^{K-1} \text{ DRP } x^K \quad (\text{i.e. } x^1 \text{ is IRP to } x^K)$$
then it must be the case that:
$$x^K \text{ is NOT strictly revealed preferred to } x^1 \quad (\text{i.e. } p^K \cdot x^1 \geq p^K \cdot x^K \text{ is required})$$
Intuition: GARP prevents cycles of revealed preferences, including those that arise indirectly across multiple intermediate purchasing decisions.
Logical Relationship: WARP vs. GARP
$$\boxed{\text{GARP} \implies \text{WARP}}$$
  • WARP tests only direct revealed preferences ($K = 2$).
  • GARP generalizes this to both direct and indirect revealed preferences ($K \geq 2$).
  • Two goods: WARP and GARP are mathematically identical.
  • More than two goods: GARP can detect cyclical inconsistencies across chains of choices that WARP completely misses.
1
Hypotheses:
Let $u$ be a locally non-satiated utility function. Suppose each bundle $x^k$ maximizes utility subject to prices $p^k$, for all $k = 1, \dots, K$.
2
Revealed Preference Chain:
Suppose $p^l \cdot x^{l+1} \leq p^l \cdot x^l$ for all $l \leq K-1$. Because each $x^l$ maximizes $u$ facing $p^l$, and $x^{l+1}$ was affordable, it follows that:
$$u(x^1) \geq u(x^2) \geq \dots \geq u(x^K)$$
3
Proof by Contradiction:
Suppose GARP is violated. Then $x^K \text{ SRP } x^1$, which means:
$$p^K \cdot x^1 < p^K \cdot x^K$$
4
Apply Local Non-Satiation (LNS):
Since $p^K \cdot x^1 < p^K \cdot x^K$, bundle $x^1$ is strictly inside the interior of budget set $K$. By local non-satiation, in any arbitrarily small neighborhood of $x^1$, there exists a bundle $\tilde{x}$ such that:
$$u(\tilde{x}) > u(x^1) \geq u(x^K)$$
Because $p^K \cdot x < p^K \cdot x^K$ is an open condition, we can choose $\tilde{x}$ close enough to $x^1$ such that:
$$p^K \cdot \tilde{x} < p^K \cdot x^K$$
This means $\tilde{x}$ was affordable under prices $p^K$ and yields strictly higher utility than $x^K$! This contradicts the fact that $x^K$ maximizes utility at $p^K$.
Therefore, $p^K \cdot x^1 \geq p^K \cdot x^K$ must hold. GARP is satisfied. $\blacksquare$
Afriat’s Theorem (The Fundamental Equivalence)
Let $D = \{(p^k, x^k)\}_{k=1}^K$ be a finite dataset of observed prices and chosen bundles. The following statements are strictly equivalent:
  1. The finite dataset $D$ satisfies GARP.
  2. The dataset $D$ can be rationalized by a locally non-satiated utility function.
  3. The dataset $D$ can be rationalized by a continuous, concave, and strictly monotone utility function.
$$\boxed{\text{Dataset satisfies GARP} \iff \text{Rationalizable by a neoclassical utility function}}$$
Economic Intuition: GARP is both necessary and sufficient to treat the consumer as if she is maximizing a well-behaved neoclassical utility function. No stronger axiom is needed.
4. Measuring Violations of GARP & Money Pump
Beyond Binary Rationality: While GARP provides a binary diagnostic — either the consumer's dataset satisfies it or it fails — real-world consumption data almost always exhibits occasional violations. Economists therefore need a quantitative measure to assess the economic severity and cost of being irrational.
Numerical Example: A 2-Period Violation of GARP
Consider a consumer observed across two price situations:
  • Period A: Prices are $p_A = (10, 15)$. The consumer chooses bundle $A = (5, 5)$, even though bundle $B = (8, 2)$ was affordable:
    $$p_A \cdot A = 10(5) + 15(5) = 125 > 110 = 10(8) + 15(2) = p_A \cdot B$$
    Since $A$ was chosen and $B$ was strictly cheaper, $A$ is revealed preferred to $B$ ($A \text{ RP } B$).
  • Period B: Later, prices change to $p_B = (14, 12)$. The consumer chooses bundle $B = (8, 2)$, even though bundle $A = (5, 5)$ was affordable:
    $$p_B \cdot B = 14(8) + 12(2) = 136 > 130 = 14(5) + 12(5) = p_B \cdot A$$
    $B$ is revealed preferred to $A$ ($B \text{ RP } A$).

We observe $A \text{ RP } B$ and $B \text{ RP } A$ with strict inequalities: this is a direct violation of GARP.

The Money Pump Cost ($mp$)
What is the economic cost of this inconsistency?
  • At prices $p_A$, the consumer is willing to spend 15 more on $A$ than on $B$: $p_A \cdot (A - B) = 125 - 110 = 15$.
  • At prices $p_B$, the consumer is willing to spend 6 more on $B$ than on $A$: $p_B \cdot (B - A) = 136 - 130 = 6$.
This generates a total money-pump cost of:
$$mp = p_A \cdot (A - B) + p_B \cdot (B - A) = 15 + 6 = 21$$
Economic Intuition: An arbitrageur could trade bundle $B$ for bundle $A$ at prices $p_A$, charging 15, and then trade bundle $A$ back for bundle $B$ at prices $p_B$, charging 6. The round-trip cycle $B \to A \to B$ leaves the consumer with the exact same initial bundle $B$, but extracts 21 in cash!

General Definitions: Money Pump Cost & Money Pump Index (MPI)
Given an observed sequence $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n$ of chosen bundles ($n$ observations) exhibiting a GARP violation cycle ($x_1 \text{ RP } x_2 \text{ RP } \dots \text{ RP } x_n$ and $x_n \text{ RP } x_1$):
  • Money Pump Cost ($mp$):
    $$mp = \sum_{h=1}^n p_h \cdot (x_h - x_{h+1}) \quad (\text{with } x_{n+1} \equiv x_1)$$
  • Money Pump Index (MPI): A normalized index expressing the monetary loss as a proportion of total expenditure:
    $$mpi = \frac{mp}{\sum_{h=1}^n p_h x_h}$$

Empirical Application: In panel datasets of household food consumption:

  • About 80% of households violate GARP at least once.
  • However, these violations represent on average only 6% of household food expenditure ($mpi \approx 0.06$).
  • Conclusion: Violations are economically small, suggesting behavior is broadly consistent with rationality perturbed by modest measurement errors or minor taste shocks.
5. Experimental Tests of Rationality
Application 1: Is Altruism Rational?

Economists observe frequent non-selfish behavior in laboratory and field experiments. Is giving to others an "irrational" deviation from economics?

  • Theoretical Insight: Altruism is completely consistent with economic rationality if it can be represented by a standard, well-behaved preference ordering satisfying GARP.
  • Experimental Design: Subjects allocate a budget between their own account ($\pi_o$, price normalized to 1) and an anonymous stranger's account ($\pi_s$, varying relative price).
  • Findings:
    • Only 10% of participants violated WARP or GARP.
    • For the 90% consistent subjects, their choices correspond to classic utility functional forms:
      $$u(\pi_s, \pi_o) = \pi_o \quad (\textbf{Purely Selfish})$$ $$u(\pi_s, \pi_o) = \min\{\pi_s, \pi_o\} \quad (\textbf{Leontief / Equal Split})$$ $$u(\pi_s, \pi_o) = \pi_s + \pi_o \quad (\textbf{Perfect Substitutes: give all if price } < 1, \text{ keep all otherwise})$$
  • Implication: Altruistic behavior is highly rational, consistent, and predictable using standard consumer theory.
Application 2: Are We Irrational by Mistake?

Are observed violations of rationality deliberate deviations from neoclassical axioms, or simply human errors?

A violation is defined as a mistake if the individual violates an axiom, but would not have done so had they been aware of the inconsistency.
  • Experimental Test: Subjects first choose whether an algorithmic decision-rule following axioms should make choices on their behalf, then make choices themselves, and finally are confronted with their revealed inconsistencies.
  • Results:
    • 85% desire an axiom algorithm to govern choices on their behalf.
    • When shown their inconsistencies: 47% actively change choices to restore consistency, 13% stop using the algorithm, and 40% maintain choices.
  • Normative Implication: Violations stem predominantly from mistakes rather than intentional anti-rational preferences. This preserves the strong normative appeal of economic theory, even if descriptive accuracy is imperfect.
Application 3: Afriat’s Critical Cost Efficiency Index & Rationality Across Age
Afriat’s Critical Cost Efficiency Index (CCEI):
The CCEI is the largest fraction $e^* \in [0, 1]$ such that, if all observed budgets are shrunk to $e^* \cdot (p \cdot x)$, the modified dataset satisfies GARP.
• $\text{CCEI} = 1$: Choices are perfectly consistent with GARP.
• $\text{CCEI} = 0.90$: Choices could be rationalized by a utility function if budgets were adjusted by at most 10%.
Intuition: Shrinking the budget set eliminates near-boundary affordable alternatives, measuring the degree of budget waste.
  • Testing Children vs. Adults: Children (7 and 11 years old) and young adults (21 years old) made repeated consumption bundle decisions (juice and snacks).
  • Proportion with Zero GARP Violations:
    • 7-year-olds: ~25%
    • 11-year-olds: ~60%
    • 21-year-olds: ~65%
  • Across all groups, mean CCEI is near 0.95.
  • Conclusion: Rational choice behavior develops significantly between ages 7 and 11, stabilizing by early adolescence. Cognitive development is a key determinant of decision consistency.
Application 4: Heterogeneity in Rationality & Socio-Demographics

Evaluating CCEI consistency in large representative population samples (Dutch household panel) across financial risk decisions reveals substantial heterogeneity:

  • Low-income individuals leave 3.3 percentage points more earnings on the table relative to high-income individuals.
  • Low-educated individuals leave 2.6 percentage points more.
  • Older individuals leave 5.1 percentage points more.

Policy Implication: Laboratory measures of revealed preference consistency directly reflect real-world financial decision-making capacity. Substantial heterogeneity in choice consistency provides economic justification for gentle choice architecture and soft "paternalism" (nudges, smart defaults).

6. Synthesis: The Three Bridges of Choice Theory
We have now completed the foundations of choice theory by establishing three core links:
  1. Preferences & Utility: Is a preference relation $\succeq$ representable by a utility function? (Debreu Theorem, continuity, monotonicity, convexity).
  2. Utility & Choice Rules: Are observed choice correspondences $C(A)$ rationalizable by some utility function? (Property $\alpha$, WARP).
  3. Preferences & Observed Market Choices: How can underlying preferences be recovered and tested using market budget data? (Direct and indirect revealed preferences, GARP, Afriat’s Theorem).
