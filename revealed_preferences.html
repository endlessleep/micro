5. Consumer’s Choices: Revealed Preferences

From observed choices to WARP, SARP and GARP, followed by the money-pump example and experimental applications.

Preamble: How Does It Work?

Chapter 4 starts from preferences and imposes assumptions on them. Here we work in the other direction: observe what the consumer chooses when other bundles are affordable, then ask whether these choices can come from one stable preference relation.

The Fundamental Inference:
Let $(x_1, x_2)$ and $(y_1, y_2)$ be two bundles, and $(p_1, p_2)$ be the prices, such that:
$$p\cdot x\geq p\cdot y$$
We observe that the consumer has chosen to buy $(x_1, x_2)$.

What can you infer about this consumer’s preferences?
She bought $x$, and $y$ would have cost no more than $x$ at those same prices. She could therefore have bought $y$ instead. Assuming that she chooses a most preferred affordable bundle, her choice tells us:
$$(x_1, x_2) \succeq (y_1, y_2)$$
Core principle: If the consumer buys a bundle instead of another affordable one, then the first bundle is revealed preferred to the second.

Reading the expenditure comparisons. The expression $p\cdot x=p_1x_1+p_2x_2$ is the total cost of bundle $x$: multiply each quantity by its price, then add the costs. When comparing $x$ and $y$, use the same prices for both. Later, $(p^k,x^k)$ denotes observation $k$: the prices faced and the bundle chosen. The comparisons below use observed expenditure as the budget.

Weak Comparison
Revealed Preferences (RP)
Consider $(y_1, y_2)$ a different bundle from $(x_1, x_2)$.

$(x_1, x_2)$ is revealed preferred to $(y_1, y_2)$ iff:
$$p\cdot x\geq p\cdot y$$
The choice of $x$ is part of the definition: $y$ was affordable when $x$ was chosen. Maximization implies $u(x)\geq u(y)$; it does not by itself exclude indifference.
Strict Comparison
Strict Revealed Preferences (SRP)
Consider $(y_1, y_2)$ a different bundle from $(x_1, x_2)$.

$(x_1, x_2)$ is strictly revealed preferred to $(y_1, y_2)$ iff:
$$p\cdot x>p\cdot y$$
Bundle $y$ was strictly cheaper when $x$ was chosen: buying $y$ would have left some of the expenditure on $x$ unspent. Under utility maximization and local non-satiation, this reveals a strict preference for $x$ over $y$.
1. Weak Axiom of Revealed Preferences (WARP)
An Example & Motivation:
The previous intuition relies on the assumption that the consumer is always choosing the most preferred bundle she can afford, i.e. maximizing her utility.

What kind of observation would lead us to conclude that the consumer is not following the utility-maximizing model?
If choices are unique, choosing distinct bundles $x$ and $y$ while both are affordable on both occasions is inconsistent: each choice would have to be strictly better than the other. If utility maximization allows ties, however, mutual affordability alone need not be a contradiction. This distinction matters when we compare WARP and GARP.
Weak Axiom of Revealed Preference (WARP)
Let $x$ be chosen at prices $p$, and a different bundle $y$ at prices $q$. WARP requires:
$$p\cdot x\geq p\cdot y \implies q\cdot y<q\cdot x.$$

The premise says that $y$ was affordable when $x$ was chosen. The conclusion says that $x$ must be outside the expenditure budget when $y$ is chosen. Equivalently, the reverse inequality $q\cdot y\geq q\cdot x$ is forbidden.

WARP checks consistency between two observed choices. We use the convention for unique choices: even equality in the reverse expenditure comparison is excluded when $x\neq y$.
Theorem: Utility Maximization and WARP
If a consumer’s observed choices come from maximizing a strictly quasi-concave utility function under budget constraint, then these observed choices satisfy WARP.

Starting point: The same strictly quasiconcave utility function $u$ is maximized on each convex budget set, and the observed maximizers exist.
Goal: For $x\neq y$, show that $p\cdot x\geq p\cdot y$ implies $q\cdot y<q\cdot x$.

1
Use uniqueness of the optimum. Strict quasiconcavity on a convex budget set ensures that the utility-maximizing bundle, when it exists, is unique. Thus a chosen bundle gives strictly higher utility than any distinct affordable bundle.
2
Use the first choice. Since $p\cdot y\leq p\cdot x$, $y$ belongs to the first budget. Optimality gives $u(x)\geq u(y)$. Equality would make $y$ a second maximizer, so uniqueness and $x\neq y$ give $u(x)>u(y)$.
3
Use the second choice. If $q\cdot x\leq q\cdot y$, then $x$ is affordable when $y$ is chosen. Optimality would give $u(y)\geq u(x)$, contradicting step 2. Consequently,
$$q\cdot x>q\cdot y.$$
This is exactly WARP. $\blacksquare$
Can We Say: Choices Satisfy WARP ⇒ Utility Maximization?
We have shown that strictly quasiconcave utility maximization implies WARP.
Does satisfying WARP guarantee utility rationalization?

Under standard consumer-demand assumptions:
  • Yes, if there are only two goods ($n = 2$).
  • Not in general with three or more goods ($n \geq 3$).

How must we strengthen WARP to obtain a theory of revealed preference that is equivalent to the theory of utility maximization?
WARP tests pairs directly. With more goods, a chain can return to its starting bundle without any pair violating WARP. SARP rules out these longer cycles between distinct bundles; GARP allows indifference while excluding cycles with a strict revealed comparison.

2. Strong Axiom of Revealed Preference (SARP)
Pairwise Comparison
Directly Revealed Preferences (DRP)
Consider $(y_1, y_2)$ a different bundle from $(x_1, x_2)$.

$(x_1, x_2)$ is directly revealed preferred to $(y_1, y_2)$ ($X \text{ DRP } Y$) iff:
$$p\cdot x\geq p\cdot y$$
$X$ was chosen when $Y$ was available and affordable under the same budget constraint.
Transitive Sequence
Indirectly Revealed Preferences (IRP)
Suppose $x^1$ is chosen when $x^2$ is affordable, and $x^2$ is chosen on another occasion when $x^3$ is affordable. The two observations form a chain. More generally:
$$\begin{gathered} x^1\ \text{DRP}\ x^2\ \text{DRP}\ \cdots\ \text{DRP}\ x^K\\ \implies x^1\ \text{IRP}\ x^K. \end{gathered}$$
Each link uses the prices at which its first bundle was chosen. Under utility maximization, the chain gives $u(x^1)\geq\cdots\geq u(x^K)$. Here IRP includes a direct link as the shortest possible chain.
Strong Axiom of Revealed Preference (SARP)
If $x^1$ is indirectly revealed preferred to a distinct bundle $x^K$, the last bundle cannot be directly revealed preferred to the first:
$$\begin{gathered} x^1\ \text{IRP}\ x^K,\quad x^1\neq x^K\\ \implies p^K\cdot x^K<p^K\cdot x^1. \end{gathered}$$

SARP applies the logic of WARP to an entire chain. The starting bundle must be unaffordable at the end, so the chain cannot close into a cycle between distinct bundles.

Relation to WARP: SARP includes the pairwise consistency required by WARP and extends it to comparisons connected through intermediate choices.
3. Generalized Axiom of Revealed Preference (GARP)
Definition — Generalized Axiom of Revealed Preference (GARP)
For any sequence of bundles $\{x^1, \dots, x^K\}$ chosen at prices $\{p^1, \dots, p^K\}$:

If:
$$x^1\ \text{IRP}\ x^K$$
then it must be the case that:
$$\text{Not }x^K\ \text{SRP}\ x^1$$
which requires:
$$p^K \cdot x^1 \geq p^K \cdot x^K$$
Intuition: The reverse comparison may hold with equality, but not strictly. A cycle consisting entirely of ties can be consistent with indifference; a cycle containing a strict revealed comparison cannot.
SARP: exclude reverse affordability
For distinct endpoints of a revealed-preference chain:
$$p^K\cdot x^1>p^K\cdot x^K.$$
The first bundle must be strictly outside the last expenditure budget.
GARP: allow equality
For the same chain:
$$p^K\cdot x^1\geq p^K\cdot x^K.$$
Equality is allowed, so the two bundles may both maximize utility.
Keep the conventions consistent. SARP implies WARP and GARP. With the unique-choice definition of WARP used above, GARP need not imply WARP: it permits ties that WARP excludes. Conversely, WARP alone need not imply GARP with three or more goods, because it does not test longer chains.
Theorem & Proof: Utility Maximization (with LNS) ⟹ GARP
If a consumer’s observed choices come from maximizing a locally non-satiated utility function under budget constraint, then these observed choices satisfy GARP.

Starting point: Each $x^k$ maximizes the same locally non-satiated utility $u$ in its observed expenditure budget, with positive prices $p^k$.
Goal: Given a chain from $x^1$ to $x^K$, prove $p^K\cdot x^1\geq p^K\cdot x^K$.

1
Translate each link into utility. For $j=1,\ldots,K-1$, direct revealed preference means $p^j\cdot x^{j+1}\leq p^j\cdot x^j$. Hence $x^{j+1}$ was feasible when $x^j$ was chosen, and optimality gives $u(x^j)\geq u(x^{j+1})$. Combining these inequalities:
$$u(x^1)\geq u(x^2)\geq\cdots\geq u(x^K).$$
2
Suppose the GARP inequality fails. Then the first bundle is strictly cheaper than the last chosen bundle:
$$p^K\cdot x^1<p^K\cdot x^K.$$
Buying $x^1$ at the last prices would leave some money unspent. There is therefore room for a sufficiently small change in quantities while staying within that budget.
3
Apply local non-satiation. LNS provides a strictly preferred bundle $\tilde{x}$ as close to $x^1$ as needed. Because $x^1$ costs strictly less than the budget, we can choose this improvement close enough to remain affordable:
$$\begin{gathered} p^K\cdot\tilde{x}<p^K\cdot x^K,\\ u(\tilde{x})>u(x^1)\geq u(x^K). \end{gathered}$$
The last comparison uses the utility ranking obtained in step 1.
4
Obtain the contradiction. At the last observation, $\tilde{x}$ is affordable and gives strictly higher utility than the chosen bundle $x^K$. This contradicts utility maximization. The assumed violation is therefore impossible, so GARP holds. $\blacksquare$
Afriat’s Theorem
For a finite dataset of nonnegative bundles and strictly positive prices, the following statements are equivalent:
  1. A finite dataset $D$ satisfies GARP.
  2. A finite dataset $D$ can be rationalized by a locally non-satiated utility function.
  3. A finite dataset $D$ can be rationalized by a continuous, concave, and strictly monotone utility function.
Intuition: GARP is both necessary and sufficient for utility rationalization with LNS. The proof above establishes necessity. The converse says that passing the test guarantees the existence of such a utility function; it does not identify a unique utility function or prove that the consumer consciously solves an optimization problem.
4. Research Focus: Violations & Money Pump
While GARP provides a binary outcome — either the data satisfies it or it does not — it is important to measure the severity of violations.
Two bundles, two price observations
The bundles are $A=(5,5)$ and $B=(8,2)$. First, the consumer chooses $A$ at prices $p_A=(10,15)$:
$$\begin{aligned} p_A\cdot A&=10(5)+15(5)\\ &=50+75=125,\\ p_A\cdot B&=10(8)+15(2)\\ &=80+30=110. \end{aligned}$$

Since $110<125$, $B$ was strictly cheaper. Thus $A$ is strictly revealed preferred to $B$.

Next, the consumer chooses $B$ at prices $p_B=(14,12)$:

$$\begin{aligned} p_B\cdot B&=14(8)+12(2)\\ &=112+24=136,\\ p_B\cdot A&=14(5)+12(5)\\ &=70+60=130. \end{aligned}$$

Since $130<136$, $A$ was strictly cheaper on this occasion. Hence $B$ is strictly revealed preferred to $A$. The cycle has strict comparisons, so GARP is violated.

What is the economic cost of the inconsistency?
At the first prices, choosing $A$ instead of $B$ costs $125-110=15$ extra. At the second prices, choosing $B$ instead of $A$ costs $136-130=6$ extra. Adding the expenditure differences gives:
$$\begin{aligned} mp&=p_A\cdot(A-B)+p_B\cdot(B-A)\\ &=(125-110)+(136-130)\\ &=15+6=21. \end{aligned}$$
The hypothetical cycle moves from $B$ to $A$ and back to $B$, paying a positive amount at each step. It ends at the starting bundle with 21 less in money. This is a potential cost of the inconsistency, not evidence that a trader actually extracted this amount.

Money Pump Cost & Money Pump Index (MPI)
Consider a cycle of observed bundles $x^1,\ldots,x^n$, with $x^{n+1}=x^1$. At each observation $h$, the next bundle is affordable:
$$p^h\cdot x^h\geq p^h\cdot x^{h+1}.$$

Each expenditure difference is nonnegative. If at least one is strictly positive, the cycle violates GARP and its money-pump cost is positive:

$$mp=\sum_{h=1}^n p^h\cdot(x^h-x^{h+1}).$$

For positive total expenditure, dividing by what was spent across the observations makes costs comparable across different spending levels:

$$mpi=\frac{mp}{\sum_{h=1}^n p^h\cdot x^h}.$$

In the two-bundle example:

$$mpi=\frac{21}{125+136}=\frac{21}{261}\approx 8.05\%.$$

Application in the course: About 80% of households violate GARP at least once, while the reported average cost is around 6% of food expenditure. Violations can therefore be frequent yet economically small. Their size matters alongside their number.

Research Focus: Experimental Applications

These applications use the same distinction: a preference describes what someone values; a revealed-preference test asks whether their choices are consistent with those values.

Is Altruism Rational?

Design. Participants allocate a budget between their own payoff $\pi_o$ and a stranger’s payoff $\pi_s$. The price of their own payoff is normalized to 1; the price $p_s$ of the stranger’s payoff varies. Each decision therefore reveals a choice from a different budget.

Result. About 10% violate WARP or GARP; about 90% pass. Several familiar utility functions describe patterns observed among the participants:

$$\begin{aligned} u(\pi_s,\pi_o)&=\pi_o,\\ u(\pi_s,\pi_o)&=\min\{\pi_s,\pi_o\},\\ u(\pi_s,\pi_o)&=\pi_s+\pi_o. \end{aligned}$$

The first values only one’s own payoff. The second values equal payoffs: increasing only the larger payoff leaves the minimum unchanged. The third values total payoff: allocate to the stranger when $p_s<1$, to oneself when $p_s>1$, and any split is optimal when $p_s=1$.

Lesson: Caring about another person can be consistent with rational choice. Rationality does not require selfishness.
Are Violations Mistakes?

A violation is a mistake if the person would have avoided it had they understood that it contradicted an axiom they wanted to follow.

Design. Participants first decide whether to let an axiom-based algorithm guide their choices. They then make choices and are shown inconsistencies. They can revise their choices, stop using the algorithm, or leave both unchanged.

Results. Around 85% initially want an axiom to guide their choices. When shown inconsistencies, 47% revise their choices, 13% stop using the algorithm, and 40% change neither.

Lesson: Some observed violations reflect errors that people wish to correct. An axiom may therefore remain useful as a guide even when it does not describe every observed choice.
Age & the Critical Cost Efficiency Index (CCEI)

The CCEI measures how much the expenditure comparisons must be relaxed to remove GARP violations. It ranges from 0 to 1. A score of 1 means that the original observations satisfy GARP; a score close to 1 means that only a small adjustment is needed.

To understand the adjustment, imagine reducing the spending threshold used to decide which alternatives count as affordable. Fewer alternatives then enter the revealed-preference comparisons, which can remove an inconsistency. A score of 0.9 corresponds to a 10% relaxation of those comparisons. The observed choices stay fixed: the adjustment concerns the consistency test.

Results. In the course’s sample of children and undergraduates, about 25% of seven-year-olds, 60% of eleven-year-olds and 65% of young adults have no GARP violations. Yet CCEI is around 0.95 in all three groups.

Lesson: The frequency of violations differs across ages, while their economic size can remain small. Counting violations and measuring their severity answer different questions.
Who Makes More Consistent Choices?

Design. A sample of 1,182 individuals makes 25 allocation decisions under risk. Choice consistency is measured using CCEI.

Results. The reported efficiency gaps are about 3.3 percentage points for lower-income participants, 2.6 for less-educated participants, and 5.1 for older participants, relative to their respective comparison groups.

Lesson: Consistency varies across individuals and is associated with socioeconomic characteristics. These comparisons describe associations; they do not by themselves establish why the differences arise.
