4. Consumer’s Preferences: Reasonable Assumptions

Monotonicity, convexity, homotheticity, separability, and numéraire properties — with their formal mathematical links to utility functions.

Preamble
From General Choice to Consumer Theory:
We started with the most general notion of rational choice over an abstract set of alternatives $X$. Now, we specialize to consumer theory, where the setting consists of bundles of goods, market prices, and budget constraints. This concrete environment motivates additional, highly structured assumptions on preferences:
  • Approach 1: Make reasonable assumptions about preferences and derive optimal choice behavior.
  • Approach 2: Observe market choices and infer underlying preference relations — the revealed-preferences approach (developed in Chapter 5).
Recall & Motivation
Recall that a rational and continuous preference relation $\succeq$ is representable by a continuous utility function $u$. Consequently, preferences can be represented graphically by indifference curves. Yet, to make progress in economic modeling and empirical analysis, general rationality and continuity are rarely enough: it is almost always necessary to make additional assumptions that restrict the shape of indifference curves. We investigate the five canonical assumptions: Monotonicity, Convexity, Homotheticity, Separability, and Numéraire properties.
1. Monotonicity
Strict Monotonicity
A preference relation $\succeq$ on $X \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n_+$ is strictly monotone if, for any two bundles $x, y \in X$:
$$x \geq y \quad\text{and}\quad x \neq y \implies x \succ y$$
Intuition: More is always strictly better. If bundle $x$ contains at least as much of every good as bundle $y$, and strictly more of at least one good, the consumer strictly prefers $x$ to $y$.
Monotonicity (Weak Monotonicity)
A preference relation $\succeq$ on $X$ is monotone (or weakly monotone) if, for any two bundles $x, y \in X$:
$$x \geq y \implies x \succeq y$$
Intuition: More is always at least as good as less. Free disposal of surplus goods ensures that additional quantities never harm the consumer.
Local Non-Satiation (LNS)
A preference relation $\succeq$ on $X$ is locally non-satiated if, for every bundle $x \in X$ and every radius $\varepsilon > 0$:
$$\exists y \in X \quad\text{such that}\quad \|x - y\| < \varepsilon \quad\text{and}\quad y \succ x$$
Intuition: For any consumption bundle $x$, no matter how wealthy the consumer already is, there exists another bundle $y$ at an arbitrarily small Euclidean distance $\varepsilon$ that is strictly preferred to $x$. This strictly rules out the existence of a "bliss point" (a global optimum where the consumer is completely satiated and refuses any change).

Hierarchy of Assumptions

Strict Monotonicity ⇒ Monotonicity ⇒ Local Non-Satiation (LNS)
Economic Intuition for: Monotonicity ⇒ Local Non-Satiation
  • Weak monotonicity: If we give you more of at least one good and no less of any other good, the new bundle is always at least as good as the old one. If preferences are continuous and strictly positive marginal utility exists in at least one direction, adding a tiny amount $\varepsilon / 2$ to any coordinate immediately produces a strictly preferred bundle within the $\varepsilon$-ball.
  • Local non-satiation: Guarantees only that it is always possible to find some nearby bundle that is strictly preferred. It does not require that giving you more of any given good improves utility. The better bundle could involve giving up a little of one good to obtain another.
Counter-Example: Locally Non-Satiated but NOT Weakly Monotone
Let $x_1$ represent money (numéraire good) and $x_2$ represent cups of coffee consumed in a morning. Consider the utility function:
$$u(x_1, x_2) = x_1 - (x_2 - 1)^2$$

Here, the consumer's ideal coffee intake is exactly 1 cup. Let us verify both properties:

  • Locally non-satiated: Take any bundle $(x_1, x_2)$ and any $\varepsilon > 0$. Consider the bundle $y = (x_1 + \frac{\varepsilon}{2}, x_2)$. The Euclidean distance is $\|x - y\| = \frac{\varepsilon}{2} < \varepsilon$, and: $$u(y) = x_1 + \frac{\varepsilon}{2} - (x_2 - 1)^2 = u(x) + \frac{\varepsilon}{2} > u(x)$$ Thus $y \succ x$. Local non-satiation holds everywhere.
  • Not weakly monotone: Compare bundle $A = (1, 1)$ and bundle $B = (1, 2)$. Clearly $B \geq A$ since $1 \geq 1$ and $2 \geq 1$. However: $$u(1, 1) = 1 - (1 - 1)^2 = 1$$ $$u(1, 2) = 1 - (2 - 1)^2 = 0$$ Since $u(1, 2) = 0 < 1 = u(1, 1)$, we have $A \succ B$. Giving more coffee strictly harms the consumer, so weak monotonicity fails!
2. Convexity
Convex Preferences
A preference relation $\succeq$ is convex if and only if, for any bundles $x, y, z \in X$ and any weight $t \in [0, 1]$:
$$x \succeq z \quad\text{and}\quad y \succeq z \implies t x + (1 - t) y \succeq z$$
Intuition: Individuals prefer "diversity", or prefer averages to extremes. If an agent considers both bundle $x$ and bundle $y$ to be at least as good as a baseline bundle $z$, then any weighted average (convex combination) of $x$ and $y$ is also at least as good as $z$.
Strict Convexity
A preference relation $\succeq$ is strictly convex if and only if, for any two distinct bundles $x \neq y$ and any $t \in (0, 1)$:
$$x \succeq z \quad\text{and}\quad y \succeq z \quad\text{and}\quad x \neq y \implies t x + (1 - t) y \succ z$$
Strict convexity rules out flat linear segments on indifference curves. Any strict mixture between two distinct indifferent bundles is strictly preferred to the endpoints.
Direct Implication of Convexity:
If a consumer strictly prefers $x$ to $y$ ($x \succ y$), then for any $t \in [0, 1]$:
$$x \succ y \implies t x + (1 - t) y \succeq y$$

Proof: Since $x \succ y \implies x \succeq y$, and by reflexivity $y \succeq y$, setting $z = y$ in the definition of convexity immediately yields $t x + (1 - t) y \succeq y$.
Intuition: Any weighted mixture between an attractive bundle $x$ and a worse bundle $y$ remains at least as good as the worse bundle $y$.

3. Homotheticity
Homothetic Preferences
A preference relation $\succeq$ is homothetic if, for any two bundles $x, y \in X$ and for every positive scalar $\lambda > 0$:
$$x \succeq y \iff \lambda x \succeq \lambda y$$
Intuition: Preferences are scalable. The relative ranking between two bundles does not depend on the scale of consumption. If you prefer 5 Olives to 7 Pretzels, scaling up your budget implies you also prefer 500 Olives to 700 Pretzels. Geometrically, the marginal rate of substitution (MRS) is constant along any ray originating from the origin.

5. Separability
Definition of Separability
Partition the universe of goods into two subsets $X$ and $Y$. The preference relation $\succeq$ on $X \times Y$ is separable if, for every $x, x' \in X$ and every $y, y' \in Y$:
$$(x, y) \succeq (x', y) \iff (x, y') \succeq (x', y')$$
Intuition: Preferences over goods in set $X$ can be analyzed completely independently of the consumption levels of goods in set $Y$. This is a crucial foundational assumption in empirical work: it allows an econometrician to estimate consumer demand for food or cars without having to simultaneously observe the consumer's labor supply, retirement savings, or leisure time.
Course Problem: Why is this preference relation non-separable?
Consider the observed ranking:
$$(\text{wine}; \text{cheese}) \succ (\text{wine}; \text{pretzels}) \succ (\text{beer}; \text{pretzels}) \succ (\text{beer}; \text{cheese})$$

Let $X = \{\text{cheese}, \text{pretzels}\}$ (food) and $Y = \{\text{wine}, \text{beer}\}$ (beverage). Test the separability condition:

1. Evaluate under $y = \text{wine}$ ($y \in Y$):
$$(\text{wine}; \text{cheese}) \succ (\text{wine}; \text{pretzels}) \implies \text{cheese} \succ \text{pretzels}$$
When drinking wine, the consumer strictly prefers cheese over pretzels.
2. Evaluate under $y' = \text{beer}$ ($y' \in Y$):
$$(\text{beer}; \text{pretzels}) \succ (\text{beer}; \text{cheese}) \implies \text{pretzels} \succ \text{cheese}$$
When drinking beer, the consumer strictly prefers pretzels over cheese!

Conclusion: Because the preference ranking over food items $X$ reverses depending on which beverage $y$ is consumed, the ranking over $X$ is not independent of $Y$. The preference relation is non-separable.

6. Numéraire Properties
The Numéraire Good: Fix good $t \in \mathbb{R}$ as the numéraire (a designated benchmark good against which all other goods are priced, typically interpreted as money or liquid wealth). Let $y \in Y$ represent the vector of all other consumption goods.
The Three Numéraire Axioms
A preference relation $\succeq$ satisfies the numéraire properties if:
  1. 1. The numéraire good is valuable:
    $$(t, y) \succeq (t', y) \iff t \geq t'$$
    Intuition: Holding other goods $y$ constant, having strictly more money $t$ is strictly better.
  2. 2. Compensation is possible:
    $$\forall y, y' \in Y, \quad \exists t \in \mathbb{R} \quad\text{such that}\quad (0, y) \sim (t, y')$$
    Intuition: Every possible non-monetary alternative $y$ has a well-defined monetary equivalent for the individual. The utility difference between alternatives can be fully measured by the consumer's Willingness to Pay (WTP).
  3. 3. No wealth effect:
    $$(t, y) \succeq (t', y') \iff (t + d, y) \succeq (t' + d, y') \quad \forall d \in \mathbb{R}$$
    Intuition: Adding or subtracting an identical amount of wealth $d$ to both bundles leaves their relative ranking completely unchanged. Income shocks do not distort relative preferences over $y$.
Comparison: No Wealth Effect vs. Separability
  • Separability: The internal ranking over goods $y$ does not depend on the level of $t$. However, the trade-off (marginal rate of substitution) between money $t$ and good $y$ might still vary with wealth $t$. Geometrically, indifference curves do not cross, but they are not necessarily parallel vertical shifts.
  • No wealth effect: Adding the same amount of wealth $t$ to two bundles preserves their exact ordering. The marginal willingness to pay for $y$ is entirely independent of wealth. Geometrically, indifference curves are exact parallel vertical shifts along the $t$-axis.
$$\boxed{\text{No wealth effect} \implies \text{Separability}}$$
"No wealth effect" is the strictly stronger assumption. It implies separability, but separability does not imply absence of wealth effects.
Equivalence with Quasi-Linear Utility
A preference relation $\succeq$ satisfies the numéraire properties:
$$\succeq \text{ satisfies the numéraire properties} \iff u(t, y) = t + v(y) \quad\text{for some } v: Y \to \mathbb{R}$$
The consumer's preferences are representable by a quasi-linear utility function (linear in money $t$, nonlinear in other goods $y$).
Proof (⇒): Assuming the 3 Numéraire Properties
1
Step 1: Construct the valuation function $v(y)$ using Compensation (Property 2).
Fix an arbitrary benchmark bundle $\bar{y} \in Y$. By Property 2 (Compensation is possible), for any bundle $y \in Y$, there exists a real number $v(y) \in \mathbb{R}$ representing the amount of numéraire that exactly compensates the agent for receiving $y$ instead of $\bar{y}$:
$$(0, y) \sim (v(y), \bar{y})$$
Similarly, for any other bundle $y' \in Y$, there exists $v(y') \in \mathbb{R}$ such that:
$$(0, y') \sim (v(y'), \bar{y})$$
2
Step 2: Apply No Wealth Effect (Property 3).
By Property 3, adding any amount of money $t$ to both sides of the indifference $(0, y) \sim (v(y), \bar{y})$ preserves indifference:
$$(t, y) \sim (t + v(y), \bar{y})$$
Likewise, adding $t'$ to $(0, y') \sim (v(y'), \bar{y})$ yields:
$$(t', y') \sim (t' + v(y'), \bar{y})$$
3
Step 3: Establish the preference relation using Transitivity.
By transitivity of $\succeq$, comparing $(t, y)$ and $(t', y')$ is strictly equivalent to comparing their monetary equivalents at the benchmark $\bar{y}$:
$$(t, y) \succeq (t', y') \iff (t + v(y), \bar{y}) \succeq (t' + v(y'), \bar{y})$$
4
Step 4: Conclude using Numéraire is Valuable (Property 1).
By Property 1, since the non-monetary good is fixed at $\bar{y}$, preferences are strictly monotone in money:
$$(t + v(y), \bar{y}) \succeq (t' + v(y'), \bar{y}) \iff t + v(y) \geq t' + v(y')$$
Combining this with Step 3 gives:
$$(t, y) \succeq (t', y') \iff t + v(y) \geq t' + v(y')$$
This proves that the numerical assignment $u(t, y) = t + v(y)$ represents the preference relation $\succeq$. $\blacksquare$
Proof (⇐): Assuming $u(t, y) = t + v(y)$
✓
Straightforward Verification of the 3 Properties:
  • Property 1: $(t, y) \succeq (t', y) \iff u(t, y) \geq u(t', y) \iff t + v(y) \geq t' + v(y) \iff t \geq t'$.
  • Property 2: Given $y, y'$, choose $t = v(y) - v(y')$. Then $u(0, y) = v(y) = t + v(y') = u(t, y')$, so $(0, y) \sim (t, y')$.
  • Property 3: $(t + d, y) \succeq (t' + d, y') \iff (t + d) + v(y) \geq (t' + d) + v(y') \iff t + v(y) \geq t' + v(y') \iff (t, y) \succeq (t', y')$. $\blacksquare$
