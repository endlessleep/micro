4. Consumer’s Preferences: Reasonable Assumptions

Monotonicity, convexity, homotheticity, separability, and numéraire properties — with their formal mathematical links to utility functions.

Preamble
From General Rational Choice to Consumer Theory:
We started with the most general notion of rational choice over an abstract set of alternatives $X$. Now let us specialize to consumer theory, where the setting (bundles of goods, prices, budgets) motivates additional assumptions on preferences and opens the door to revealed-preference analysis.

Two complementary approaches:
  • Make reasonable assumptions about preferences and derive choice behavior.
  • Observe choices and infer underlying preference relations — the revealed-preferences approach (studied in Chapter 5).
Recall & Mathematical Setting
Recall that a rational continuous preference relation $\succeq$ is representable by a continuous utility function $u$. In other words, preferences can be represented by indifference curves.

Yet, to make progress in economic research, it is almost always necessary to make additional assumptions that restrict preferences. Let us examine the five most commonly used assumptions:
  • Monotonicity: Increasing quantities does not make the consumer worse off.
  • Convexity: Averages are preferred to extremes (diversity in consumption).
  • Homotheticity: Scaling both bundles by the same positive factor preserves their ranking.
  • Separability: The ranking within one group of goods is independent of the common quantities of other goods.
  • Numéraire properties: Valuation and compensation can be measured through money.
1. Monotonicity

Let $X=\mathbb{R}_+^n$ be the consumption set. A bundle $x=(x_1,\ldots,x_n)$ lists quantities of goods. The inequality $x\geq y$ is componentwise: $x_i\geq y_i$ for every good $i$. Together with $x\neq y$, it means strictly more of at least one good.

Strict Monotonicity
$\succeq$ is strictly monotone if:
$$x \geq y \quad\text{and}\quad x \neq y \implies x \succ y$$
Intuition: More is always better. If bundle $x$ contains at least as much of every good as bundle $y$, and strictly more of at least one good, then $x$ is strictly preferred to $y$.
Monotonicity (Weak Monotonicity)
$\succeq$ is monotone if:
$$x \geq y \implies x \succeq y$$
Intuition: More is always at least as good as less. Extra quantities of goods never reduce utility.
Local Non-Satiation (LNS)
$\succeq$ is locally non-satiated if, for all $x \in X$ and all $\varepsilon > 0$:
$$\exists y \in X:\quad \begin{aligned} \|x-y\| &< \varepsilon,\\ y &\succ x. \end{aligned}$$
Intuition: For any bundle $x$, there is another bundle $y$ at an arbitrarily small distance $\varepsilon$ that is strictly preferred to $x$. The improvement must be available in every neighbourhood, however small. Ruling out a best bundle somewhere in $X$ would not, by itself, be enough.
What follows from strict monotonicity?

On $\mathbb{R}_+^n$, strict monotonicity implies both weak monotonicity and local non-satiation.

Starting point: Rational, strictly monotone preferences on $\mathbb{R}_+^n$.
Goal: Establish weak monotonicity and LNS.

1
Weak monotonicity. Take $x\geq y$. If $x\neq y$, strict monotonicity gives $x\succ y$, hence $x\succeq y$. If $x=y$, reflexivity gives $x\succeq y$. These two cases cover every $x\geq y$.
2
Find a nearby improvement. Fix any $x\in X$ and $\varepsilon>0$. Increase only the first coordinate:
$$y=(x_1+\varepsilon/2,x_2,\ldots,x_n).$$
This bundle remains in $X$. Its Euclidean distance from $x$ is $\varepsilon/2<\varepsilon$. Since $y\geq x$ and $y\neq x$, strict monotonicity gives $y\succ x$. This proves LNS. $\blacksquare$
A qualification to the implication in the slides. With the weak definition above, monotonicity alone does not imply LNS. For instance, $u(x)=0$ is weakly increasing: more goods never lower utility. Yet no bundle has strictly higher utility, so LNS fails. A strict improvement assumption is needed for that implication.
Course Problem · Counter-Example Monotonicity vs. LNS
Find an example of a utility function which is locally non-satiated but not weakly monotone.
Economic Interpretation
Think of $x_1$ as money (general purchasing power, where more is always strictly better) and $x_2$ as cups of coffee (where more coffee is not always better beyond 1 cup).
Utility Function
$$u(x_1, x_2) = x_1 - (x_2 - 1)^2$$
For a fixed amount of money, utility is highest at $x_2=1$. More coffee beyond this point reduces utility.
1. Locally Non-Satiated (LNS) Satisfied ✓

Take any bundle $(x_1, x_2)$ and any radius $\varepsilon > 0$. Consider the bundle:

$$y = \left(x_1 + \frac{\varepsilon}{2}, \; x_2\right)$$

Distance is $\|y - x\| = \frac{\varepsilon}{2} < \varepsilon$, and evaluating utility yields:

$$\begin{aligned} u(y)&=x_1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}-(x_2-1)^2\\ &=u(x)+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\\ &>u(x). \end{aligned}$$

Adding an arbitrarily small amount to money $x_1$ makes the consumer strictly better off.

2. Weak Monotonicity Violated ✗

Compare bundle $A = (1, 1)$ and bundle $B = (1, 2)$. Clearly $B \geq A$ componentwise ($1 \geq 1$ and $2 \geq 1$).

$$u(1, 1) = 1 - (1 - 1)^2 = 1$$ $$u(1, 2) = 1 - (2 - 1)^2 = 0$$

Since $u(1, 2) = 0 < 1 = u(1, 1)$, we have:

$$(1,1)\succ(1,2)$$

Giving more coffee strictly harms the consumer, so weak monotonicity is violated!

Conclusion: $u(x_1, x_2) = x_1 - (x_2 - 1)^2$ is locally non-satiated but not weakly monotone. LNS therefore does not imply weak monotonicity either: an improvement in money is always possible, even though more coffee can be undesirable.
2. Convexity
Convex Preferences
On a convex consumption set, $\succeq$ is convex if, for all bundles $x,y,z$ and every $t\in[0,1]$:
$$\begin{gathered} x\succeq z,\quad y\succeq z\\ \implies tx+(1-t)y\succeq z. \end{gathered}$$
Intuition: Individuals prefer “diversity”, or prefer averages to extremes. If a consumer likes $x$ at least as much as $z$, and $y$ at least as much as $z$, then any mixture (weighted average) between $x$ and $y$ is at least as good as $z$.
Strict Convexity
For distinct bundles $x\neq y$ and an interior weight $t\in(0,1)$, strict convexity strengthens the conclusion:
$$\begin{gathered} x\succeq z,\quad y\succeq z\\ \implies tx+(1-t)y\succ z. \end{gathered}$$
When $x\sim y$, a genuine mixture is strictly preferred to both endpoints. If the endpoints are not indifferent, the definition only guarantees an improvement over their common lower benchmark $z$, not over the better endpoint. The restrictions $x\neq y$ and $0<t<1$ exclude mixtures that simply reproduce an endpoint.
An Implication of Convexity:
$$x\succ y \implies tx+(1-t)y\succeq y$$

Intuition: If the consumer strictly prefers $x$ to $y$, then any weighted average of $x$ and $y$ is at least as good as the worse bundle $y$.
Argument, for any $t\in[0,1]$: First, $x\succ y$ implies $x\succeq y$. Second, reflexivity gives $y\succeq y$. Thus both endpoints are at least as good as the benchmark $z=y$. Applying convexity gives $tx+(1-t)y\succeq y$.

3. Homotheticity
Homothetic Preferences
$\succeq$ is homothetic if, for any $\lambda > 0$:
$$x \succeq y \iff \lambda x \succeq \lambda y$$
Intuition: Multiply every coordinate of both bundles by the same positive number: their ranking is unchanged. If $(5,0)\succeq(0,7)$, then $(500,0)\succeq(0,700)$. The reverse comparison follows by scaling back by $1/\lambda$. This concerns proportional changes in all goods, not an increase in just one good.
4. Link to $u$ Representation
Figure 1: Representation of an indifference curve.
This figure illustrates how monotonicity, convexity, and homotheticity translate into the geometry of indifference curves:
  • Strict monotonicity: Along an indifference curve, more of one good must be offset by less of the other.
  • Convexity: The curve bows inward toward the origin, meaning upper contour sets are convex.
  • Homotheticity: When this additional assumption holds, proportional expansions of one indifference curve generate others; it cannot be inferred from a single curve alone.

A utility representation translates rankings into inequalities. Convexity corresponds to quasiconcavity: the utility of a mixture is at least the lower of the two endpoint utilities. Homogeneity is a property of a suitably chosen representation; an arbitrary increasing transformation need not preserve it.

5. Separability
Definition of Separability
$\succeq$ is separable if, for every $x, x' \in X$ and every $y, y' \in Y$:
$$\begin{gathered} (x,y)\succeq(x',y)\\ \iff (x,y')\succeq(x',y'). \end{gathered}$$
Intuition: Compare $x$ with $x'$ while holding the other goods fixed at a common level $y$. Replacing that common level by $y'$ must leave the ranking unchanged. For example, separability can make the ranking of consumption bundles independent of a fixed level of leisure; it does not remove the consumer’s budget constraint.
Course Problem: Why is the following preference relation non-separable?
Consider the observed ranking:
$$\begin{aligned} (\text{cheese},\text{wine}) &\succ(\text{pretzels},\text{wine})\\ &\succ(\text{pretzels},\text{beer})\\ &\succ(\text{cheese},\text{beer}). \end{aligned}$$

Let $X = \{\text{cheese}, \text{pretzels}\}$ (food) and $Y = \{\text{wine}, \text{beer}\}$ (beverages):

1. When drinking wine ($y = \text{wine}$):
The first comparison gives $(\text{cheese},y)\succ(\text{pretzels},y)$. The consumer prefers cheese to pretzels when the beverage is wine.
2. When drinking beer ($y' = \text{beer}$):
The last comparison gives $(\text{pretzels},y')\succ(\text{cheese},y')$. The consumer now prefers pretzels, so the same food ranking is not preserved.

Conclusion: The ranking over food $X$ reverses depending on the beverage $Y$ chosen. Preferences over $X$ cannot be studied independently of $Y$: the relation is non-separable.

6. Numéraire Properties
The Numéraire Good: Let us fix $t$ as the numéraire good. Write an alternative as $(t,y)\in\mathbb{R}\times Y$, where $t$ is money and $y$ describes the other goods. Allowing $t$ to be any real number lets us describe both payments and compensation.
The Three Numéraire Properties
A preference relation $\succeq$ satisfies the numéraire properties if:
  1. 1. The numéraire good is valuable:
    $$(t, y) \succeq (t', y) \iff t \geq t'$$
    Intuition: More of $t$ is better.
  2. 2. Compensation is possible: For every $y,y'\in Y$, there exists $t\in\mathbb{R}$ such that
    $$(0,y)\sim(t,y')$$
    Intuition: Every possible alternative has a monetary value for the individual. It implies that the “utility” of different alternatives can be measured by the willingness of the agent to pay for it.
  3. 3. No wealth effect:
    $$\begin{gathered} (t,y)\succeq(t',y')\\ \implies (t+d,y)\succeq(t'+d,y') \end{gathered}$$
    Intuition: For every $d\in\mathbb{R}$, adding the same amount to both alternatives preserves their ranking, even when their initial money amounts differ. Applying the property with $-d$ also gives the reverse implication.
What is the difference between assuming No Wealth Effect and Separability?
Separability

The ranking over $y$ does not depend on a common money level $t$. This compares alternatives with the same amount of money; the money needed to compensate for a change in $y$ may still depend on wealth.

No wealth effect

Adding the same amount to two alternatives preserves their ranking even if their initial money amounts differ. Monetary compensation for a change in $y$ is therefore independent of the initial wealth level.

$$\begin{gathered} \text{No wealth effect}\\ \implies \text{Separability in }y \end{gathered}$$

Why this implication holds: Start with $(t,y)\succeq(t,y')$ and add $d=s-t$ to both alternatives. This gives $(s,y)\succeq(s,y')$. Adding $-d$ gives the converse. Thus the ranking of $y$ and $y'$ is independent of their common money level.

Equivalence: Numéraire Properties ⟺ Quasi-Linear Utility
For a rational preference relation on $\mathbb{R}\times Y$, the three numéraire properties hold if and only if there exists a utility representation of the form
$$u(t,y)=t+v(y),\qquad v:Y\to\mathbb{R}.$$
The utility function $u : \mathbb{R} \times Y \to \mathbb{R}$ is quasi-linear.
Starting Point / Hypotheses:
We assume rational preferences on $\mathbb{R}\times Y$ satisfying the three numéraire properties: (1) $t$ is valuable, (2) Compensation is possible, and (3) No wealth effect.
Goal: Construct $v:Y\to\mathbb{R}$ so that $u(t,y)=t+v(y)$ represents the preference relation.
1
Step 1: Construct $v(y)$ using Compensation (Property 2).
Fix an arbitrary reference bundle $\bar{y} \in Y$. Because compensation is possible, for every $y \in Y$, there exists $v(y) \in \mathbb{R}$, the money amount that makes the reference alternative $\bar{y}$ equivalent to $y$:
$$(0, y) \sim (v(y), \bar{y})$$
This amount is unique: if two different amounts were equivalent to $(0,y)$, transitivity would make them indifferent at $\bar y$, contradicting the strict value of money. Similarly, for $y'\in Y$:
$$(0, y') \sim (v(y'), \bar{y})$$
2
Step 2: Add wealth using No Wealth Effect (Property 3).
Indifference means that both weak comparisons hold. Apply property 3 to each comparison, adding $t$ to both alternatives:
$$(t, y) \sim (t + v(y), \bar{y})$$
Similarly, adding $t'$ preserves indifference:
$$(t', y') \sim (t' + v(y'), \bar{y})$$
3
Step 3: Compare bundles via Transitivity.
By transitivity of $\succeq$, comparing $(t, y)$ and $(t', y')$ is equivalent to comparing their monetary equivalents at $\bar{y}$:
$$\begin{gathered} (t,y)\succeq(t',y')\\ \iff (t+v(y),\bar y)\succeq(t'+v(y'),\bar y). \end{gathered}$$
In each direction, replace the two bundles by their indifferent monetary equivalents and use transitivity.
4
Step 4: Apply Numéraire is Valuable (Property 1).
Because the numéraire good is valuable (Property 1), the last comparison can be rewritten purely in terms of the numéraire amounts:
$$\begin{gathered} (t,y)\succeq(t',y')\\ \iff t+v(y)\geq t'+v(y'). \end{gathered}$$
Hence $\succeq$ can be represented by the quasi-linear utility function $u(t, y) = t + v(y)$. $\blacksquare$
Starting Point / Hypotheses:
We assume preferences are represented by a quasi-linear utility function $u(t, y) = t + v(y)$ for some $v: Y \to \mathbb{R}$.
Goal: Verify each of the three numéraire properties.
1
Property 1 Verification (Numéraire is valuable):
By utility representation:
$$\begin{gathered} (t,y)\succeq(t',y)\\ \iff t+v(y)\geq t'+v(y)\\ \iff t\geq t'. \end{gathered}$$
The last step subtracts the same number $v(y)$ from both sides.
2
Property 2 Verification (Compensation is possible):
For any $y, y' \in Y$, choose $t = v(y) - v(y') \in \mathbb{R}$. Then:
$$\begin{aligned} u(t,y')&=t+v(y')\\ &=[v(y)-v(y')]+v(y')\\ &=v(y)=u(0,y). \end{aligned}$$
Since $u(0, y) = u(t, y')$, it follows immediately that $(0, y) \sim (t, y')$. $\checkmark$
3
Property 3 Verification (No wealth effect):
For any displacement $d \in \mathbb{R}$:
$$\begin{aligned} u(t+d,y)&=u(t,y)+d,\\ u(t'+d,y')&=u(t',y')+d. \end{aligned}$$
Adding the same number $d$ to two utility levels preserves their order. Thus $(t,y)\succeq(t',y')$ if and only if $(t+d,y)\succeq(t'+d,y')$. All 3 properties are fully satisfied. $\blacksquare$
