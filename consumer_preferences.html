4. Consumer’s Preferences: Reasonable Assumptions

Monotonicity, convexity, homotheticity, separability, and numéraire properties — with their formal mathematical links to utility functions.

Preamble
From General Rational Choice to Consumer Theory:
We started with the most general notion of rational choice over an abstract set of alternatives $X$. Now let us specialize to consumer theory, where the setting consists of bundles of goods, market prices, and budget constraints.
This concrete setting motivates additional assumptions on preferences and opens the door to revealed-preference analysis:
  • Approach 1: Make reasonable assumptions about preferences and derive choice behavior.
  • Approach 2: Observe choices and infer underlying preference relations — the revealed-preferences approach (developed in Chapter 5).
Recall & Motivation
Recall that a rational continuous preference relation $\succeq$ is representable by a continuous utility function $u$. In other words, preferences can be represented by indifference curves. Yet, to make progress in economic research, it is almost always necessary to make additional assumptions that restrict preferences. Let us examine the most commonly used assumptions: Monotonicity, Convexity, Homotheticity, Separability, and Numéraire properties.
1. Monotonicity
Strict Monotonicity
A preference relation $\succeq$ on $X \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n_+$ is strictly monotone if:
$$x \geq y \quad\text{and}\quad x \neq y \implies x \succ y$$
Intuition: More is always better. If bundle $x$ contains at least as much of every good as $y$, and strictly more of at least one good, then $x$ is strictly preferred to $y$.
Monotonicity (Weak Monotonicity)
A preference relation $\succeq$ on $X$ is monotone if:
$$x \geq y \implies x \succeq y$$
Intuition: More is always at least as good as less. Extra quantities never harm the consumer (free disposal).
Local Non-Satiation (LNS)
A preference relation $\succeq$ on $X$ is locally non-satiated if, for all $x \in X$ and all $\varepsilon > 0$:
$$\exists y \in X \quad\text{such that}\quad \|x - y\| < \varepsilon \quad\text{and}\quad y \succ x$$
Intuition: For any bundle $x$, there is another bundle $y$ at a small Euclidean distance $\varepsilon$ that is strictly preferred to $x$. This rules out a "best bundle" (bliss point) beyond which you do not want more of anything.

Hierarchy of Assumptions

Strict Monotonicity ⇒ Monotonicity ⇒ Local Non-Satiation
Intuition for: Monotonicity ⇒ Local Non-Satiation
  • Weak monotonicity: If I give you more of at least one good and no less of any other good, the new bundle is always at least as good as the old one.
  • Local non-satiation: It is always possible to find a nearby bundle (combination of goods) that is better. But it does not guarantee that giving you more of any good is always better.
Example: Locally Non-Satiated but NOT Weakly Monotone
Think of $x_1$ as money and $x_2$ as cups of coffee, with utility function:
$$u(x_1, x_2) = x_1 - (x_2 - 1)^2$$

Here, more coffee is not always better (ideal intake is 1 cup):

  • Locally non-satiated: From any $(x_1, x_2)$, adding an arbitrarily small amount $\frac{\varepsilon}{2} > 0$ to $x_1$ yields $u(x_1 + \frac{\varepsilon}{2}, x_2) = u(x_1, x_2) + \frac{\varepsilon}{2} > u(x_1, x_2)$, making the consumer strictly better off.
  • Not weakly monotone: Notice that $(1, 2) \geq (1, 1)$, but:
    $$u(1, 2) = 1 - (2 - 1)^2 = 0 < 1 = 1 - (1 - 1)^2 = u(1, 1)$$
    Hence $(1, 2) \succeq (1, 1)$ does not hold! More coffee reduces satisfaction.
2. Convexity
Convex Preferences
$\succeq$ is convex if and only if, for any $t \in [0, 1]$:
$$x \succeq z \quad\text{and}\quad y \succeq z \implies t x + (1 - t) y \succeq z$$
Intuition: Individuals prefer “diversity”, or prefer averages to extremes. If a consumer likes $x$ at least as much as $z$, and $y$ at least as much as $z$, then any mixture (weighted average) between $x$ and $y$ is at least as good as $z$.
Strict Convexity
Preferences are strictly convex if and only if, for any $t \in (0, 1)$:
$$x \succeq z \quad\text{and}\quad y \succeq z \quad\text{and}\quad x \neq y \implies t x + (1 - t) y \succ z$$
An Implication of Convexity:
If the consumer strictly prefers $x$ to $y$, then for any $t \in [0, 1]$:
$$x \succ y \implies t x + (1 - t) y \succeq y$$
Intuition: If the consumer strictly prefers $x$ to $y$, then any weighted average of $x$ and $y$ is at least as good as the worse bundle $y$. (Proof: $x \succ y \implies x \succeq y$, and by reflexivity $y \succeq y$; setting $z = y$ in the convexity definition immediately yields the result).
3. Homotheticity
Homothetic Preferences
$\succeq$ is homothetic if, for any $\lambda > 0$:
$$x \succeq y \iff \lambda x \succeq \lambda y$$
Intuition: Preferences are scalable. If you prefer 5 Olives to 7 Pretzels, you also prefer 500 Olives to 700 Pretzels. Geometrically, the marginal rate of substitution is invariant along rays originating from the origin.

Local Non-Satiation $u$ has no local maximum on $\mathrm{Int}(X)$ Thick indifference curves are ruled out.

5. Separability
Definition of Separability
$\succeq$ is separable if, for every $x, x' \in X$ and every $y, y' \in Y$:
$$(x, y) \succeq (x', y) \iff (x, y') \succeq (x', y')$$
Intuition: Preferences over goods $X$ can be studied independently from the levels of goods on other sets. This is a key assumption in empirical research on consumer behavior. It implies that you can study consumers’ choices independently of their labor/leisure choices.
Course Problem: Why is the following preference relation non-separable?
Consider the preference relation:
$$(\text{wine}; \text{cheese}) \succ (\text{wine}; \text{pretzels}) \succ (\text{beer}; \text{pretzels}) \succ (\text{beer}; \text{cheese})$$

Let $X = \{\text{cheese}, \text{pretzels}\}$ and $Y = \{\text{wine}, \text{beer}\}$.

• With $y = \text{wine}$: $(y; \text{cheese}) \succ (y; \text{pretzels}) \implies \text{cheese} \succ \text{pretzels}$.
• With $y' = \text{beer}$: $(y'; \text{pretzels}) \succ (y'; \text{cheese}) \implies \text{pretzels} \succ \text{cheese}$.

Answer: The preference ranking over food $X$ reverses depending on the beverage $Y$ consumed. Therefore, preferences over $X$ are not independent of $Y$, which violates separability!

6. Numéraire Properties
The Numéraire Good: Let us fix $t$ as the numéraire good. The numéraire is just a good you can measure everything else against. Think of it as money.
The 3 Numéraire Properties
A preference relation $\succeq$ is said to satisfy the numéraire properties if the following hold:
  1. 1. The numéraire good is valuable:
    $$(t, y) \succeq (t', y) \iff t \geq t'$$
    Intuition: More of $t$ is better.
  2. 2. Compensation is possible:
    $$\text{For every } y, y', \quad \exists t \in \mathbb{R} \quad\text{such that}\quad (0, y) \sim (t, y')$$
    Intuition: Every possible alternative has a monetary value for the individual. It implies that the “utility” of different alternatives can be measured by the willingness of the agent to pay for it.
  3. 3. No wealth effect:
    $$(t, y) \succeq (t', y') \implies (t + d, y) \succeq (t' + d, y') \quad \forall d \in \mathbb{R}$$
    Intuition: Changes in $t$ do not affect preferences over $y$.
No Wealth Effect & Separability: What is the difference?
  • Separability: The ranking over $y$ does not depend on $t$, but the trade-off between $t$ and $y$ may depend on the level of $t$. The indifference curves do not cross but are not necessarily parallel.
  • No wealth effect: Adding the same amount of $t$ to two alternatives does not change their ranking. The indifference curves are parallel shifts in $t$.
$$\boxed{\text{No wealth effect} \implies \text{Separability}}$$
Link to $u$ representation: Quasi-Linear Utility
A preference relation $\succeq$ satisfies the numéraire properties:
$$\succeq \text{ satisfies the numéraire properties} \iff u(t, y) = t + v(y) \quad\text{for some } v: Y \to \mathbb{R}$$
The utility function $u : \mathbb{R} \times Y \to \mathbb{R}$ is quasi-linear.
Proof ($\implies$):
1
Because compensation is possible: There exists $v(y)$, the amount of the numéraire good that compensates the agent for getting $y$ instead of a benchmark $\bar{y}$:
$$(0, y) \sim (v(y), \bar{y})$$
Similarly, for any other bundle $y'$:
$$(0, y') \sim (v(y'), \bar{y})$$
2
Because there is no wealth effect: Adding $t$ and $t'$ preserves indifference:
$$(t, y) \sim (t + v(y), \bar{y})$$
Similarly:
$$(t', y') \sim (t' + v(y'), \bar{y})$$
3
Equivalence by Transitivity: Hence:
$$(t, y) \succeq (t', y') \iff (t + v(y), \bar{y}) \succeq (t' + v(y'), \bar{y}) \quad (*)$$
4
Because the numéraire good is valuable: Condition $(*)$ can be rewritten as:
$$(t, y) \succeq (t', y') \iff t + v(y) \geq t' + v(y')$$
Hence $\succeq$ can be represented by the quasi-linear utility function $u(t, y) = t + v(y)$. $\blacksquare$
Proof ($\impliedby$):
✓
$\impliedby$ is straightforward:
  • 1. $(t, y) \succeq (t', y) \iff t + v(y) \geq t' + v(y) \iff t \geq t'$.
  • 2. For any $y, y'$, set $t = v(y) - v(y')$; then $u(0, y) = v(y) = t + v(y') = u(t, y')$, so $(0, y) \sim (t, y')$.
  • 3. $(t + d) + v(y) \geq (t' + d) + v(y') \iff t + v(y) \geq t' + v(y')$. $\blacksquare$
